# Two pacing fixes, both measured before and after

Two numbers from yesterday's breakdown were wrong in ways a reader would feel.

## What we fixed

**Lab 1 was the thinnest page on the site, and it is the front door.** 1,006
words against a ten-lab average of ~1,430 — it was written first, as the
prototype, before the form had been calibrated, and the nine that followed came
out longer. It now has the section it was missing: **how the matrix is laid out
in memory**, and why that layout is the same reading the essay already gives.

`Mat4` is a `Float32Array` of sixteen in column-major order, so the array is
literally the x axis, then the y axis, then the z axis, then the position —
four contiguous groups of four. That is why `translation(x, y, z)` lands its
arguments at indices 12, 13 and 14; why `gl.uniformMatrix4fv(location, false, m)`
hands the array over untouched; and why the readout above it, printed in rows,
is the transpose of what sits in the buffer. The figure renders the same matrix
both ways at once, with the cells that still match the identity dimmed, so at
full slider exactly five of sixteen light up.

1,006 → 1,495 words. No longer the shortest; that is now pipeline at 1,088.

**The compute lab made you read 386 words before anything appeared** — the worst
opening on the site against a median of 230. It now opens on a diagram
contrasting the three shader stages by *who calls them*: a vertex shader because
something is about to be rasterised, a fragment shader because something is about
to be blended, and a compute shader because you asked, with nothing downstream
waiting. Only the compute row is tinted, so the break in the pattern is the thing
you see.

386 → 90 words. Now the best opening on the site.

## What we checked

Both agents verified their claims against the source rather than asserting them.
The transform one caught a sign asymmetry worth keeping: `rotationY` writes `+sin`
at `m[8]` and `-sin` at `m[2]`, so an earlier draft calling them "its two sines"
read as though they were the same number.

One claim in the new section is a spec fact rather than a repo fact — that WebGL 1
rejects a non-false transpose flag. It is correctly scoped to WebGL 1 (WebGL 2
relaxes it), and the repo-checkable half is verified: every canvas on that page
goes through `GLCanvas`, which requests a WebGL 1 context, and every call site
passes `false`.

## Key decisions

- **Not trimming to hit a number.** The transform section came in slightly over
  the band I asked for, and every sentence in it carries a checkable fact. Cutting
  good sentences to land on an approximate word count would have been the wrong
  trade.
- **Leaving the nested `<figure>` alone.** `MatrixView` renders its own `figure`
  inside the essay's, which is legal HTML and predates all of this. Changing a
  shared component for a semantic nit is not worth the risk to nine other pages.

# The one sentence nothing checked

Lab 1's new section tells the reader that `gl.uniformMatrix4fv(location, false, m)`
hands the array over untouched, and that in WebGL 1 — "which every canvas on this
page runs on" — the `false` is not a choice, because passing `true` is an error.

The spec half of that is not this repo's to prove. The two premises underneath it
are, and nothing checked either: that the canvases really are WebGL 1, and that
nothing anywhere uploads a matrix transposed. Switch `GLCanvas` to a `webgl2`
context, or pass `true` at one of the fifteen call sites, and the essay silently
becomes wrong about the site it is describing.

Two checks now, in the shader suite. One walks every `uniformMatrix4fv` call in
`lib/` and `components/`, balancing parentheses to read the real argument list
rather than matching the prose that quotes the signature, and asserts the
transpose flag is `false`. The other asserts `GLCanvas` requests `'webgl'` and
not `'webgl2'`. Both failure messages name the sentence they are protecting, so
whoever trips one knows there is prose to revisit and not just a lint to satisfy.

Mutation-tested: transposing one upload in ShadingLab fails the first, switching
GLCanvas to `webgl2` fails the second.

## Key decisions

- **Checking the premises rather than deleting the sentence.** The claim is true
  and it is the kind of detail this site exists to give. What was wrong was that
  it rested on two facts about the codebase that nothing defended.
- **Leaving the other three spec claims alone.** The shader essay's helper
  invocations, and the compute essay's two WebGPU limits, are accurate statements
  about the platform with no repo-side premise to enforce. Inventing a test for
  them would be theatre.
