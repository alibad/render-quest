# Two pacing fixes, both measured before and after

Two numbers from yesterday's breakdown were wrong in ways a reader would feel.

## What we fixed

**Lab 1 was the thinnest page on the site, and it is the front door.** 1,006
words against a ten-lab average of ~1,430 — it was written first, as the
prototype, before the form had been calibrated, and the nine that followed came
out longer. It now has the section it was missing: **how the matrix is laid out
in memory**, and why that layout is the same reading the essay already gives.

`Mat4` is a `Float32Array` of sixteen in column-major order, so the array is
literally the x axis, then the y axis, then the z axis, then the position —
four contiguous groups of four. That is why `translation(x, y, z)` lands its
arguments at indices 12, 13 and 14; why `gl.uniformMatrix4fv(location, false, m)`
hands the array over untouched; and why the readout above it, printed in rows,
is the transpose of what sits in the buffer. The figure renders the same matrix
both ways at once, with the cells that still match the identity dimmed, so at
full slider exactly five of sixteen light up.

1,006 → 1,495 words. No longer the shortest; that is now pipeline at 1,088.

**The compute lab made you read 386 words before anything appeared** — the worst
opening on the site against a median of 230. It now opens on a diagram
contrasting the three shader stages by *who calls them*: a vertex shader because
something is about to be rasterised, a fragment shader because something is about
to be blended, and a compute shader because you asked, with nothing downstream
waiting. Only the compute row is tinted, so the break in the pattern is the thing
you see.

386 → 90 words. Now the best opening on the site.

## What we checked

Both agents verified their claims against the source rather than asserting them.
The transform one caught a sign asymmetry worth keeping: `rotationY` writes `+sin`
at `m[8]` and `-sin` at `m[2]`, so an earlier draft calling them "its two sines"
read as though they were the same number.

One claim in the new section is a spec fact rather than a repo fact — that WebGL 1
rejects a non-false transpose flag. It is correctly scoped to WebGL 1 (WebGL 2
relaxes it), and the repo-checkable half is verified: every canvas on that page
goes through `GLCanvas`, which requests a WebGL 1 context, and every call site
passes `false`.

## Key decisions

- **Not trimming to hit a number.** The transform section came in slightly over
  the band I asked for, and every sentence in it carries a checkable fact. Cutting
  good sentences to land on an approximate word count would have been the wrong
  trade.
- **Leaving the nested `<figure>` alone.** `MatrixView` renders its own `figure`
  inside the essay's, which is legal HTML and predates all of this. Changing a
  shared component for a semantic nit is not worth the risk to nine other pages.

# The one sentence nothing checked

Lab 1's new section tells the reader that `gl.uniformMatrix4fv(location, false, m)`
hands the array over untouched, and that in WebGL 1 — "which every canvas on this
page runs on" — the `false` is not a choice, because passing `true` is an error.

The spec half of that is not this repo's to prove. The two premises underneath it
are, and nothing checked either: that the canvases really are WebGL 1, and that
nothing anywhere uploads a matrix transposed. Switch `GLCanvas` to a `webgl2`
context, or pass `true` at one of the fifteen call sites, and the essay silently
becomes wrong about the site it is describing.

Two checks now, in the shader suite. One walks every `uniformMatrix4fv` call in
`lib/` and `components/`, balancing parentheses to read the real argument list
rather than matching the prose that quotes the signature, and asserts the
transpose flag is `false`. The other asserts `GLCanvas` requests `'webgl'` and
not `'webgl2'`. Both failure messages name the sentence they are protecting, so
whoever trips one knows there is prose to revisit and not just a lint to satisfy.

Mutation-tested: transposing one upload in ShadingLab fails the first, switching
GLCanvas to `webgl2` fails the second.

## Key decisions

- **Checking the premises rather than deleting the sentence.** The claim is true
  and it is the kind of detail this site exists to give. What was wrong was that
  it rested on two facts about the codebase that nothing defended.
- **Leaving the other three spec claims alone.** The shader essay's helper
  invocations, and the compute essay's two WebGPU limits, are accurate statements
  about the platform with no repo-side premise to enforce. Inventing a test for
  them would be theatre.

# Driving the interface instead of loading it

## What we fixed

The 101-check rendering smoke test contains zero clicks. It proves every page
returns 200 and every canvas drew something; it has never proved that a single
control does anything. Six agents drove the live site with a real browser —
lab controls, lab chrome, site chrome, learn and glossary, the technology
chooser, and a keyboard-and-screen-reader pass — each required to show state
changing before and after, not that a click failed to throw. 219 checks, and
every failure below was reproduced by hand before anything was touched.

**The instancing lab lost your camera.** Seven labs put azimuth and elevation in
the URL. The eighth kept them in a `useRef` — the obvious choice, since the
render loop is the only reader — and the reader paid for it silently: the
copy-link button is gated on a non-empty query, so orbiting wrote nothing to the
address bar, offered no link, and reopening the page threw the framing away. The
camera now lives in the shared control state like everywhere else.

**The technology chooser argued against its own recommendation.** Every scored
reason was printed after the word "Because", regardless of sign, so three of the
twenty answer combinations justified a pick with the penalty against it — most
sharply "Start with Three.js. Because it is very good at hiding what you are
trying to learn." Reasons are now split by sign: what counts in favour after
"Because", what counts against after "Despite". Nothing is hidden; it is on the
correct side of the ledger.

**And it broke its own hard rule.** The footer promises that no amount of
preference makes WebGL an option for compute. Three.js scored −3 for a compute
goal with no sentence attached, so the penalty was invisible *and* small enough
for preference to outrun: for a compute workload the card recommended Three.js
directly above WebGL struck out with a red CANNOT badge — recommending the
renderer it had just declared impossible. The penalty is now −6, exactly the
largest bonus preference can award, and it says why.

**The glossary letter nav hid the term you asked for.** 19 of 20 letters landed
their first term at y=1px — 56px behind the sticky header — so clicking M put
Mipmap out of sight and Model matrix at the top. The scroll margin was on the
entry; the letter target was a marker nested inside it, inheriting nothing. The
letters now point at the entries' own anchors.

**The texture lab's magnification filter did nothing.** Sweeping every control
on every lab and measuring the canvas before and after turned up one that never
moved a pixel: Nearest against Linear magnification, at full resolution, on a
still frame — 0.000. Not a wiring fault. The lab opens looking down a heavily
tiled plane, "where minification bites hardest", and both filtering presets tile
it *further*, so in every state the lab offers by name every texel is smaller
than a pixel and the magnification filter has nothing to do. Its own note talks
about what happens "up close" from a view that is never up close. There is now a
preset that goes there — one tile, seen from above, where flipping the control
moves the picture by 9.1 — and when the reader is not there, the panel says so
instead of letting them conclude the lab is broken.

**Eight keyboard and screen-reader defects.** The camera was pointer-only in all
eight labs that have one — "drag to orbit" with no way to accept the invitation
from a keyboard. The `role="radiogroup"` implemented none of the radio pattern
and had no accessible name. Nine sliders announced themselves as "x", "y", "z"
three times over and four buttons as "Reset", because the headings that
disambiguate them were presentational. The search dialog dropped focus on
`<body>` when dismissed and let Tab walk out onto header controls painted
underneath it. The source-panel disclosure was the one control on the site whose
focus ring did not paint — clipped by the panel's `overflow-hidden`. And the `#`
that marks a heading as linkable appeared on hover only.

## Key decisions

- **Fix the shape, not the instance.** The control column's group headings now
  reach the accessible names through context, so no lab repeats itself at
  sixteen call sites; the arrow-key camera lives in `GLCanvas`, so all seven
  WebGL labs got it at once.
- **Two new structural guards, both mutation-tested.** A lab with a camera must
  keep it in the shareable state, and a camera must be movable from the
  keyboard. Both fail when the fix is reverted.
- **`test/chooser.test.ts` walks all twenty answer combinations.** Both chooser
  defects lived in three of them, which is exactly what a spot check misses. The
  suite asserts the promise the tool makes about itself: every point it awards
  is shown back as a sentence, on the correct side.

## Still open

The header, footer and mobile menu all link to `github.com/alibad/render-quest`,
which is **private** — every visitor gets a GitHub 404, directly beside footer
copy reading "built in the open". The markup is correct; the repository's
visibility is the bug. Publishing a repository is not reversible, so it is the
owner's call: make it public, or drop the three links.
